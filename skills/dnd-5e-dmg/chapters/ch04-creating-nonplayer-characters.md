# Chapter 4: Creating Nonplayer Characters

## Core Idea
Most NPCs need only one or two memorable qualities; a small number who matter deserve the "ten sentences" treatment — one sentence each across ten fixed categories — which reliably produces a usable, distinct character without novel-length backstory.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Ten Sentences NPC framework**: one sentence each for (1) Occupation and history, (2) Appearance, (3) Abilities (only note ones above/below average), (4) Talent, (5) Mannerism, (6) Interactions with others, (7) Useful knowledge, (8) Ideal, (9) Bond, (10) Flaw or secret.
  - When to use: any NPC who'll recur or matter more than a one-line extra. Skip entirely for "quick NPCs" — one or two memorable traits is enough for a bit player.
  - How: use the d20/d12/d6/d8/d10 tables under each category (Reference Tables) to generate fast, or hand-pick traits that suit the character.
  - Why it matters mechanically: Ideal, Bond, and Flaw/Secret aren't just flavor — a PC who *uncovers* one of these three can use it to influence the NPC in social interaction (Ch 8 rules).
- **Villain's Scheme framework**: separate the villain's Objective (Immortality / Influence / Magic / Mayhem / Passion / Power / Revenge / Wealth — d8, each with its own sub-table) from their Methods (a large d20 category table: assault, captivity/coercion, confidence scams, defamation, execution, murder, politics, religion, theft, torture, vice, warfare, etc., each with its own sub-roll). This factoring — motive separate from tactics — is the reusable trick: any objective can be paired with any method to avoid cookie-cutter villains.
- **Loyalty (optional rule)** for NPC party members: a hidden 0-20 score. Max = party's highest Charisma score; starts at half that. +1d4 for the NPC's bond being furthered or the NPC being treated well/rescued; -1d4 for acting against the NPC's alignment/bond; -2d4 for abusing/endangering the NPC selfishly. ≥10 = risks life and limb for the party; 1-9 = tenuous; 0 = no longer loyal, may leave or work against the party.
  - When to use: any recurring NPC companion, to make "does the party deserve this ally's help" mechanically real instead of assumed.

## Key Concepts
- **NPC statistics, three options**: (1) no combat stats at all — most NPCs, (2) a customized Monster Manual stat block, (3) full class-and-levels build like a PC (needed for anything that must hold its own in extended combat or has class features the story depends on).
- **Contact** — an NPC tied to a PC who supplies info/rumors/supplies/advice but doesn't adventure.
- **Patron** — a contact who employs the party and has a vested interest in their success (usually doesn't need to be persuaded to help).
- **Hireling** — paid help (guides, porters, mercenaries); distinct from a Contact in that money, not relationship, is the primary bond.
- **Extra** — background characters with no assigned importance until a player singles one out — be ready to improvise a name and mannerism on the spot.
- **Low-level follower** — a lower-level NPC apprentice/squire/acolyte a PC can take on; earns a full XP share, so gains levels faster than the main party (studying under masters) while slightly slowing the PCs' own advancement.

## Mental Models
- Treat NPC depth as **proportional to narrative weight**, not fixed effort — a blacksmith needs one tattoo, a recurring villain needs ten sentences, a *named monster* villain (e.g. a beholder crime boss) needs the full humanoid treatment (ideal/bond/flaw) layered on top of its stat block, not just its Monster Manual entry as-is.
- Separate a villain's **why** (Scheme objective) from their **how** (Methods) as two independent random-generation axes — this is the same "orthogonal axes" trick useful for any procedurally-varied NPC design.
- A villain's weakness should be **discoverable, not just declared** — treat it as a mini-side-quest (find the phylactery, learn the true name, complete the old bargain) rather than a stat block footnote.

## Anti-patterns
- **Over-detailing every NPC.** The chapter explicitly warns most NPCs need far less complexity than a novel character — reserve the ten-sentence treatment for NPCs that will recur or drive plot.
- **NPCs as automatons.** When a player-run supporting/adventurer NPC stops being roleplayed true to its traits, the DM should reclaim it, reassign it, or have it leave — don't let it become a mute resource extension of the party.
- **Making loyalty visible.** The Loyalty score is explicitly meant to be tracked in secret — telling players the number turns a roleplaying signal into a meta-game resource to min-max.
- **A weakness that's just handed to the party.** Villain weaknesses should require investigation/questing to discover, mirroring how they're concealed in-fiction (a lich hides its phylactery).

**NPC Bonds** (d10): personal life goal · protective of family · protective of colleagues · loyal to a patron/employer · captivated by a romantic interest · drawn to a special place · protective of a keepsake · protective of a valuable possession · out for revenge · (roll twice on a 10).

**NPC Flaws/Secrets** (d12): forbidden love · decadent pleasures · arrogance · envy · overpowering greed · prone to rage · has a powerful enemy · specific phobia · shameful history · secret crime · forbidden lore · foolhardy bravery.

**Villain's Scheme — Objectives** (d8, each rolls a sub-table): Immortality (d4) · Influence (d4) · Magic (d6) · Mayhem (d6) · Passion (d4) · Power (d4) · Revenge (d4) · Wealth (d4).

**Villain's Secret Weakness** (d8): soul bound to a hidden object · broken if a lost love is avenged · weakened near a specific artifact · a special weapon deals extra damage · destroyed by speaking its true name · felled per an ancient prophecy/riddle · falls if an old enemy forgives it · loses power if an old mystic bargain is completed.

**Villainous class options** (crunch, for evil NPCs/PCs with DM approval): **Death Domain** (cleric) — bonus proficiency with martial weapons, Reaper (necromancy cantrips hit one extra target), Channel Divinity: Touch of Death (bonus necrotic damage on a hit), Divine Strike (extra necrotic damage at 8th level), Improved Reaper (higher-level necromancy spells hit extra targets). **Oathbreaker** (paladin) — replaces a broken Sacred Oath; grants Channel Divinity (control undead / dreadful aspect), Aura of Hate (necrotic damage bonus to self and evil allies), Supernatural Resistance (damage resistance while raging... while oath-corrupted), and capstone Dread Lord (frightful, damage-resistant transformation). An Oathbreaker can seek Atonement to return to a standard paladin oath.

## Worked Example
Building a recurring villain with the Scheme + Weakness frameworks: roll Objective → "3, Magic" → sub-roll d6 → "3, Carry out a deity's wishes." Roll Methods → "14, Politics" → sub-roll → "1, Betrayal or treason." Combined: an evil high priest who believes their god demands the sitting monarch's overthrow, working through court betrayal rather than open war — built mechanically as a cleric with the Death Domain (per Villainous Class Options). Apply the Ten Sentences: Occupation = "Royal confessor, secretly ordained by a forbidden death cult 20 years ago"; Appearance = "unnaturally pale, wears the finest court robes"; Ideal = "Retribution (evil)"; Bond = "loyal to a patron" (the death god); Flaw = "arrogance." Weakness (d8 roll "1") = a hidden reliquary holding a fragment of their god's power — destroying it breaks the priest's Channel Divinity, giving the party a concrete objective beyond simply winning a fight.

## Key Takeaways
1. Reserve the Ten Sentences framework for NPCs whose relationships will matter — most NPCs need only one or two traits.
2. Ideal/Bond/Flaw are mechanically active, not just color — PCs who learn them gain social-interaction leverage (Ch 8).
3. Separate a villain's Objective from their Methods as independent generation axes to avoid repetitive villain design.
4. A villain's weakness should be hidden and questable, never handed over for free.
5. Use the Loyalty score (tracked secretly, 0-20, max = party's highest CHA) to make NPC companion allegiance a real, earned mechanic.
6. Named monster villains deserve the same ideal/bond/flaw/mannerism treatment as humanoid NPCs — don't run them as a bare stat block.
