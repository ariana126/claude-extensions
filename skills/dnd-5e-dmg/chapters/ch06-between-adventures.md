# Chapter 6: Between Adventures

## Core Idea
Downtime between adventures isn't dead air — it's a toolkit of concrete subsystems (crafting, business, carousing, renown, rumors, strongholds) that pace level progression, deepen investment in the world, and give characters a life outside the dungeon.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Campaign continuity discipline**: kept-dead-things-stay-dead / moved treasure doesn't respawn / opened doors stay open. Track it via notes directly on the adventure map (short-term) and a campaign notebook (long-term) with these sections: Campaign Planner, Character Notes, Player Handouts, Adventure Log, NPC Notes, Campaign Calendar, Toolbox (custom monsters/items/traps/maps you don't want to recreate).
- **Downtime activity resolution pattern**: nearly every downtime activity in this chapter follows the same shape — spend N days (scaling with settlement size, item rarity, or renown level) → make a specific ability check → consult a d100(+modifier) table for the outcome. Learning this pattern lets you build custom downtime activities on the same chassis (see "Creating Downtime Activities" guidance below).
- **Linking adventures**: use an overarching story two ways — "Quest of Many Parts" (one goal split across multiple adventures, e.g. collecting pieces of a ritual) or "Agents of X" (a recurring faction/villain behind separately-flavored adventures). Plant seeds early (a name dropped, an item glimpsed) and pay them off later via foreshadowing so the campaign feels authored, not episodic-by-accident.
- **Downtime scaling with level**: low-level campaigns pass days/weeks between adventures; high-level campaigns (with strongholds, businesses, renown to maintain) should pass months/years — downtime duration itself is a lever for signaling character growth in stature, not just power.

## Key Concepts
- **Maintenance costs** — any owned property (shop, keep, temple, palace) has a per-day upkeep cost paid every 30 days, covering staff and hireling salaries; the party needs a steward to keep this running while away.
- **Garrison** — armed defenders for a property, statted as veterans/guards (Monster Manual), scaled to property type.
- **Carousing** — spend at a wealthy lifestyle rate; roll d100+level against a results table ranging from jail time to a whirlwind romance to a small gambling fortune.
- **Crafting a magic item** — requires a formula, spellcasting ability to produce any spell the item grants, and a level minimum by rarity; progresses 25 gp of value per 8-hour work day; a spell-producing item consumes one spell slot of the spell's level per day of creation (and its material components).
- **Selling a magic item** — two-step: DC 20 Investigation check to find a buyer at all (days-to-find scale with rarity), then a d100+Persuasion roll against a payout table (a "shady buyer, no questions asked" can pay 1.5× base price). Legendary items/artifacts can't be sold via downtime — that's adventure material.
- **Sowing rumors** — DC 15 Charisma (Deception/Persuasion) after a settlement-size-scaled number of *continuous* days (interrupting resets progress) shifts a community's general attitude toward a target one step, friendly or hostile.
- **Training to gain levels (variant rule)** — gate new-level benefits behind a training period (10-40 days, 20-80 gp) scaled to the level band being entered.

## Mental Models
- Treat downtime not as "nothing happening" but as **character investment infrastructure** — a PC who owns a tavern in a village makes that village's threats matter to the player, independent of plot armor.
- Use the shared **days → check → d100 table** downtime chassis as a template: any custom downtime activity you invent should slot into this same shape rather than needing bespoke mechanics.
- Renown-based downtime (Ch 1) scales itself automatically: the time cost to gain the *next* renown point is renown × 10 days, so climbing an organization's ranks naturally slows down as a character rises — no separate diminishing-returns rule needed.

## Anti-patterns
- **Letting the world reset between sessions.** Moved treasure, dead monsters, and opened doors must stay changed — breaking this discipline undermines the sense of a persistent, real world faster than almost anything else.
- **Interrupting a rumor campaign and expecting progress to carry over.** The rules are explicit: in a town/city, the days spent sowing a rumor must be continuous, or the rumor fades and needs to restart.
- **Skipping the steward.** A property's maintenance is explicitly assumed to be handled by a steward while the party adventures — without one, don't hand-wave upkeep as automatically paid.

## Worked Example
A 6th-level PC wants to craft a rare wand (5,000 gp creation cost, min level 6 — they qualify). They also want passive income, so they buy a Guildhall (5,000 gp, 60 days construction) to run between adventures. During the 60-day build, they also spend downtime crafting: at 25 gp/day, the wand takes 200 days of dedicated work — far longer than the guildhall build — so the player realistically either pools crafting effort with allies (each contributing 25 gp/day to cut the timeline) or accepts the wand as a long-term background project the DM tracks in the campaign notebook's "Character Notes" section across multiple real sessions of in-fiction downtime.

## Key Takeaways
1. Keep a campaign notebook (Planner / Character Notes / Handouts / Adventure Log / NPC Notes / Calendar / Toolbox) — improvised continuity breaks immersion fast.
2. World state persists: dead stays dead, moved treasure doesn't respawn, opened doors stay open.
3. Nearly every downtime activity follows days-spent → check → d100 outcome table; use this chassis to invent new ones.
4. Scale downtime duration to campaign level — weeks at low level, months/years at high level — as a narrative signal of growing stature, not just a pacing knob.
5. Legendary items and artifacts are adventure hooks, not downtime-sellable commodities.
6. Renown-gated downtime (renown × 10 days per point) self-paces — no extra throttling rule needed.
