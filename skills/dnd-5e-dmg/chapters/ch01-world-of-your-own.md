# Chapter 1: A World of Your Own

## Core Idea
Your campaign world is built from five **Core Assumptions** (gods oversee the world, much of the world is untamed, the world is ancient, conflict shapes history, the world is magical) — and the most interesting worlds are made by consciously inverting one or more of them, not by accepting all five by default.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Core Assumptions inversion**: For each assumption, ask "What if the standard assumption weren't true?" (e.g. Dark Sun = gods absent/distant; Eberron = magic is everyday commerce; Ravenloft = monsters are rare but terrifying rulers). This is the primary tool for making a homebrew setting distinctive without inventing from a blank page.
  - When to use: at the very start of world creation, before any geography or history work.
  - How: pick 1-2 of the 5 assumptions to invert; let the inversion cascade into implications (e.g. "magic is mundane" → item shops are common, authorities regulate spells like weapons).
- **Tiers of Play**: campaign structure keyed to character level, used to scope how big your world-shaking events and antagonists should be at each stage.
  - Levels 1-4, "Local Heroes": 1st-2nd level spells, mundane gear, common consumables + rare uncommon items. Threats: ores, wolves, giant spiders, cultists, ghouls. Avoid young dragons.
  - Levels 5-10, "Heroes of the Realm": 3rd-level spells at tier start (fireball, lightning bolt, water breathing), 5th-level by tier end (teleportation circle, scrying, raise dead). Uncommon/rare permanent items begin appearing. Threats: giants, hydras, golems, yuan-ti, devils, demons, mind flayers. Can plausibly beat a young dragon in its early lair.
  - Levels 11-16, "Masters of the Realm": 6th-level spells at 11th (disintegrate, heal, teleport, true seeing). Rare/very rare items. Threats: lower-planar masterminds, rakshasas, beholders, purple worms, adult dragons. Characters start building strongholds, founding guilds/orders.
  - Levels 17-20, "Masters of the World": 9th-level spells (wish, gate, astral projection). Legendary items (vorpal sword, staff of the magi) begin appearing. Threats: balors, titans, archdevils, lich archmages, ancient dragons, avatars of gods.
  - When to use: to calibrate every antagonist, magic item, and stake to the party's current tier instead of guessing.
- **World-Shaking Events**: campaign-structuring device — d10 table (Rise/Fall of a Leader, Cataclysmic Disaster, Assault/Invasion, Rebellion, Extinction/Depletion, New Organization, Discovery, Prophecy, Myth/Legend) used to generate the 1-3 largest plot beats of a campaign.
  - When to use: a campaign can sustain roughly **3 large-scale world-shaking events** — one near the beginning, middle, and end. More than that and the events stop feeling significant ("false action").
  - How: roll or pick a category, then work through that category's guided questions (who benefited, who was blamed, what's the unexpected twist) to flesh it out.
- **Renown / Piety**: a numeric (starts at 0) per-faction standing track, separate from XP, that gates rank, NPC attitude, and perks within a specific organization or deity.
  - How: +1 renown for advancing an org's interests generally; +2 for completing a mission the org specifically assigned or that directly benefits it. Set rank thresholds (and optional level prerequisites) per faction. Renown never drops below 0. "Piety" is the same system reskinned for standing with a deity/pantheon, unlocking prayer-for-favor (~1/day at a rank) and eventually a persistent blessing/charm.

## Key Concepts
- **Loose pantheon** — many deities, none doctrinally united (most D&D worlds default).
- **Tight pantheon** — a single religion/dogma binding several deities, structured like a family (e.g. Norse gods); usually has "aberrant gods" — unsanctioned evil members with their own cults.
- **Mystery cult** — secretive initiation-based worship of one god within a larger pantheon; membership is deeply personal, built around a death/rebirth foundation myth.
- **Divine rank** — Greater deity (unsummonable, near-uninvolved) > Lesser deity (embodied on a plane, encounterable) > Quasi-deity (demigod/titan/vestige — divine origin but doesn't grant spells or hear prayers).
- **Continuing vs. episodic campaign** — continuing = connected adventures sharing an arc/villain; episodic = self-contained "monster of the week" structure (adventurers-for-hire premise).
- **Table scale for maps**: Province scale = 1 hex/mile (day's travel radius); Kingdom scale = 1 hex/6 miles (~Great Britain-sized); Continent scale = 1 hex/60 miles.

## Mental Models
- Think of **settlement purpose** (not size) as the design driver: a settlement is either "local color" (name + one sensory detail, done), a "home base" (invest in recurring NPCs the party cares about), or an "adventure site" (detail only the rooms/NPCs the adventure touches). Don't over-build what the plot won't use.
- Think of **play style as a spectrum**, not a binary: Hack-and-Slash (clear villains, no bookkeeping, action-first) at one end, Immersive Storytelling (rules take a back seat, sessions can pass without an attack roll) at the other. Most tables land "Something in Between" — mix roleplay and combat encounters deliberately rather than defaulting to one pole.
- Think of a campaign's **theme** the way a novel has one: pick a throughline (mortality, insidious evil, the beast within, thirst for power) and let disconnected adventures still rhyme with it, rather than needing one continuous plot to feel unified.

## Anti-patterns
- **False action**: stringing together world-shaking events with no real impact on characters/world just to keep things "epic." It desensitizes players — save large-scale stakes for the ~3 events a campaign can actually sustain.
- **Over-detailing background the players will never see**: writing 20 pages of setting lore instead of a 2-page campaign handout. Let players uncover depth through play, not a reading assignment.
- **Name-flavor mismatch**: mixing "Lord Cupcake" into a party of Sithis, Travok, and Anastrianna breaks immersion — set naming ground rules with players at session zero.
- **Ignoring core assumptions without intent**: changing "the world is magical" to false without tracing the downstream effects (item availability, spellcaster rarity, how NPCs react to magic) produces an incoherent setting.

## Worked Example
Building a homebrew world using the framework: Start with the Core Assumptions and invert one — "The World Is Magical" becomes "Magic is exceedingly rare and feared" (a Dark Sun-style inversion). Cascading implications: item shops don't exist; a single spellcaster in a village is a source of local legend, not a shopkeeper; authorities treat any spellcasting as a criminal act absent a license (see "Restrictions on Magic"). Next, pick Tier 1 (Levels 1-4) framing: threats are ores and cultists, not dragons. Sketch a Province-scale map (1 hex/mile) centered on a home base village, following the 3-step "Start Small" procedure: (1) create a home base settlement, (2) fill in a day's-travel local region (~25-30 miles) with 2-4 dungeons and 1-3 more settlements, (3) craft a single-dungeon starting adventure. Finally, pick one World-Shaking Event for the campaign's opening: roll a d10, get "7 — New Organization"; decide a secretive magic-hoarding cabal is quietly forming in the region, which becomes the campaign's opening hook and ties directly back to the "magic is rare and feared" inversion.

## Key Takeaways
1. Don't accept all 5 Core Assumptions by default — invert 1-2 deliberately to make your world distinctive, and trace the downstream implications.
2. Scope every threat, magic item, and stake to the party's current Tier of Play (1-4 / 5-10 / 11-16 / 17-20) rather than improvising difficulty.
3. A campaign can sustain about 3 world-shaking events (beginning/middle/end) — more than that becomes "false action" and stops landing.
4. Detail settlements only to the depth their narrative purpose requires: local color, home base, or adventure site.
5. Use Renown (and its Piety variant) as a separate progression track from XP/treasure to make faction and deity relationships mechanically meaningful.
6. Establish play style and naming conventions with players before the campaign starts — this is effectively a "Session Zero" alignment step, not an in-play discovery.
